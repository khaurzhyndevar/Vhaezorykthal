# Vhaezorykthal

> Beneath the basalt rose, a hornless sovereign counts the shadows of unborn moons.

## The Ninth Courtyard

Eight courtyards surround the palace. The ninth occupies the space beneath a closed hand. It contains a well, a chair made of reeds, and a rose cut from basalt. The rose has seven petals when seen from the well and six when seen from the chair.

No visitor has stood between them. The ground there is always occupied by the shadow of someone arriving tomorrow.

At dusk, the rose releases a little black pollen. The caretaker gathers it with a silver spoon and carries it into the palace. He returns with the spoon empty and his sleeves wet to the elbows. Asked where the water comes from, he describes the sound of a door being shut beneath snow.

His description varies. The snow does not.

## Concerning the Empty Crown

An empty crown hangs above the well. It is suspended by nothing visible. Its points have been wrapped in pale thread, and from each point descends a single drop of water that never falls.

Those drops contain landscapes. One holds a plain of dark grass. Another holds a stairway whose upper steps are missing. A third contains the courtyard itself, except that the basalt rose is alive and the caretaker has no shadow.

The remaining drops appear empty until a visitor closes one eye. Then each shows a different crown, and beneath every crown stands the same chair of reeds.

The caretaker discourages comparisons. On one occasion, a traveler drew all the crowns on a sheet of paper. The paper became heavy enough to break the table. Beneath the broken wood lay a patch of snow, though the afternoon was warm.

## A Fragment From the Well

The well has never held water. Its rope returns damp, and its bucket contains a smooth stone shaped like a tongue. The stone changes color whenever someone speaks beside it.

A mason once lowered a lantern into the shaft. He watched its light descend until it appeared above the crown. Below, the shaft remained dark. Above, the lantern swung gently between the hanging drops.

The mason retrieved the rope. It ended in a little root, still growing. The lantern remained overhead for thirteen evenings. On the fourteenth, it opened like a fruit, revealing a white feather folded around a tooth.

The caretaker buried both beneath the basalt rose. Since then, the pollen has carried the faint smell of rain on warm scales. The mason has stopped making doorways with straight sides.

## The Sovereign Below the Petals

The palace was built around a sleeper whose body has never been measured. Its spine lies beneath the outer courtyards. Its wings cross under the well. Where horns should rise from its head, two narrow staircases lead into darkness.

Nobody calls the sleeper a dragon within the palace. The word appears only in descriptions written beyond the walls, and those descriptions lose a letter every winter.

The sovereign has no throne. The chair of reeds is left outside for a guest who has not yet acquired a name. Whenever the sleeper turns, the chair faces a different direction. Whenever it dreams, a new petal appears in the rose's reflection.

The caretaker has been counting those reflected petals for forty years. His latest total is a small blue ribbon, folded twice, which he keeps beneath the silver spoon.

## Marks Without a Reading

Along the well's inner rim, four groups of marks become visible when no lamp is lit:

*Vhae — zory — kthal — ennur.*

A visitor who copied them discovered that her ink remained wet after seven days. She placed the page near a fire. The fire went out. She placed it in the sun. Her shadow moved into the palace without her.

It returned after dusk carrying the blue ribbon. The caretaker denied giving it away. His own ribbon remained beneath the spoon, folded three times instead of two.

The visitor tied hers around the tongue-shaped stone. A sound rose from the well, too low to be heard directly. Everyone in the courtyard remembered a different room. All the remembered rooms contained the same closed hand.

## The Unfinished Courtesy

Guests are offered a bowl of clear broth before leaving. Beneath its surface lies the reflection of an empty road. Drinking is customary. Finishing is not recorded.

If the road bends toward the palace, the guest is given a second spoon. If it bends away, the caretaker opens a door that has not previously been visible. If no road appears, a place is prepared beside the well, and the chair of reeds becomes warm.

The visitor with the ribbon received neither spoon nor door. Her bowl contained a basalt rose, small enough to rest against the bottom. She left it untouched and walked through the courtyard's shadow until she reached morning.

Behind her, the caretaker gathered black pollen. The crown counted another drop. Far beneath the petals, the hornless sleeper lifted one eyelid, and the ninth courtyard became briefly large enough to hold an unopened moon.
